It begins over the lake.

A heat dome sits on southern Ontario. Lake Ontario is bath-warm, and the air above it is loaded.

You are up here — one droplet in a storm cell the size of the city.

Warm air holds more water — about 7 % more for every degree. This air has been warming for a century.

Because this flood followed a map — one drawn long before the city existed.

A city built on water

Before Toronto, the land drained itself. 360 km of creeks, brooks and rivers wound through ravines and marshes to the lake. The first maps of York are full of water.

The creeks become sewers

The growing city used its creeks as drains. Mills, privies and slaughterhouses emptied into them; cholera and typhoid followed. The Victorian cure: put the creek in a brick pipe and sell the land on top. Lower Taddle Creek went under in 1852.

The great erasure

In 1882, a plan to keep the ravines as parkland lost at council. Within a decade Garrison Creek — all 7 km of it — was sealed into Canada’s largest brick sewer, and the lower Don was straightened, severing twelve tributaries.

Annex, survey, pipe, repeat

Every annexed town brought creeks; every creek got a culvert. After Hurricane Hazel killed 81 people in 1954, even “flood control” meant more concrete. The expressways finished the job.

293 km in the dark

By 1975, barely a stream in the old city still saw daylight. Of the 360 km mapped here, 293 km now run through pipes beneath streets, schoolyards and parking lots.

Water remembers

Bury a river and it doesn’t leave — it waits. In a cloudburst the old creeks fill first: manholes lift and basements flood along valleys the street grid pretends aren’t there. The 2024 flood traced this map.

There is another way.

Daylighting is the opposite of burial: digging a stream out of its culvert and giving it an open, planted channel again — room to run, room to flood, room to be seen.

Room for cloudbursts

An open channel with floodable banks holds many times what a buried pipe can. Parks designed to flood mean basements that don’t.

Cooler streets

Open water and the trees that line it knock degrees off summer heat that pavement only stores up.

Living corridors

Daylighted reaches see fish, herons and dragonflies return within seasons — and people follow them.

Cheaper than bigger pipes

Aging culverts fail expensively. When one must be rebuilt anyway, opening it often costs less — and yields a park instead of a buried liability.

Zurich · the Bachkonzept

Since 1988, Zurich has daylighted more than 20 km of buried brooks — partly to keep clean stream water out of its overloaded sewers. Today brooks run in the open beside tram lines, and on hot days the whole city swims in its rivers.

London · the River Quaggy

For decades the Quaggy ran in concrete culverts beneath Lewisham and Greenwich. In 2003 it was set loose into Sutcliffe Park, regraded as a wetland that is allowed to flood. The park now stores the storm water that once filled living rooms — protecting hundreds of homes — and the kingfishers are back.

Seoul demolished an expressway to free the Cheonggyecheon. Sheffield, Aarhus, Yonkers… no city that has opened a river has wanted to bury it again.
