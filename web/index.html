Medicine solved the wrong half

    There is another way.

    Toronto's inner harbour, today — at right, the Port Lands, where the Don just got its mouth and floodplain back

    Daylighting is the opposite of burial: dig the stream out of its culvert and give it back what the pipe amputated — a floodplain, soaking banks, a living channel. The park and the herons are what recovery looks like.

    Room for cloudbursts

    Parks designed to flood mean basements that don’t.

    Cooler streets

    Open water and trees knock degrees off summer heat.

    Living corridors

    Fish and herons return within seasons — people follow.

    Cheaper than bigger pipes

    When a failing culvert must be rebuilt anyway, opening it often costs less.

    Zurich · the Bachkonzept

    Since 1988 Zurich has daylighted more than 20 km of buried brooks. On hot days the whole city swims in its rivers.

    London · the River Quaggy

    In 2003 the Quaggy was let out of its concrete culvert into Sutcliffe Park — a wetland allowed to flood. It now stores the storms that once filled living rooms, and the kingfishers are back.

    Seoul · the Cheonggyecheon

    Seoul demolished a four-lane elevated expressway to set the Cheonggyecheon free. The corridor now runs 3–6 °C cooler than streets a block away.

    Toronto · the Port Lands

    Not a foreign idea. Toronto’s $1.4 billion Port Lands project carved a new wetland mouth for the Don — Canada’s largest urban river restoration, opened in 2025.

    The fine print · what it costs

    Daylighting is not free flood control: scarce land, buried utilities, years of construction, maintenance forever. The honest comparison is daylighting vs. the next bigger pipe — plus everything the pipe still can’t do.
